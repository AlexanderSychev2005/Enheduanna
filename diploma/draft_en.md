## 1. Introduction

Cuneiform tablets, among the earliest surviving written records, are frequently damaged, and reconstructing their missing content is a slow, expert-dependent process: roughly half of the half a million cuneiform tablets excavated to date remain untransliterated (Cobanoglu et al. 2024, p. 3: "About 50 percent of the roughly half a million cuneiform tablets which have been excavated so far have not yet been transliterated or published"). Where a tablet survives only in fragments, philologists reconstruct the missing signs by hand, relying on contextual cues — a process that is subjective and time-consuming (Lazar et al. 2021, p. 4682: "Due to the tablets' deterioration, scholars often rely on contextual cues to manually fill in missing parts in the text in a subjective and time-consuming process").

This reconstruction task maps directly onto masked language modeling (MLM), the pretraining objective underlying BERT: a model learns to predict a token from its surrounding context (Devlin et al. 2019, p. 4171: "BERT alleviates the previously mentioned unidirectionality constraint by using a 'masked language model' (MLM) pre-training objective, inspired by the Cloze task... The masked language model randomly masks some of the tokens from the input, and the objective is to predict the original vocabulary id of the masked [token]"). Lazar et al. (2021) were the first to frame this connection explicitly for Akkadian, as a masked-token-prediction task (p. 4683: "We identify that the longstanding challenge of filling in gaps in Akkadian texts directly corresponds to advances in masked language modeling").

A parallel line of work in Greek and Latin epigraphy has pursued the same idea with purpose-built architectures. Pythia was the first deep-learning system for ancient-text restoration, trained on Greek inscriptions (Assael et al. 2019, p. 6368: "This work presents PYTHIA, the first ancient text restoration model that recovers missing characters from a damaged text input using deep neural networks"). Its successor Ithaca extended restoration with two attribution tasks — assigning a damaged inscription's geographic origin and its date of writing — within one multi-task architecture (Assael et al. 2022, p. 280: "we present Ithaca, a deep neural network for the textual restoration, geographical attribution and chronological attribution of ancient Greek inscriptions"). Aeneas extended Ithaca further, adding a vision branch that conditions the geographic-attribution head on a photograph of the inscription, and handling gaps of unknown length (Assael et al. 2025, p. 142: "Aeneas retrieves textual and contextual parallels, leverages visual inputs, handles arbitrary-length text restoration, and advances the state of the art in key tasks"). For Akkadian, Lazar et al. (2021) established that a fine-tuned multilingual encoder outperforms a from-scratch monolingual one for restoration (p. 4686: "the two M-BERT models, which were finetuned over Oracc texts, outperform all other models by at least 20% on both metrics"), but did not extend the task to attribution or to a visual modality. We combine both lines of work in Enheduanna — named for the Akkadian high priestess and poet credited as history's earliest author known by name — a fine-tuned multilingual BERT, in the spirit of Lazar et al. (2021), extended with Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution heads (period, genre, language, provenience) and an Aeneas-style vision-conditioned head, applied to Akkadian for the first time.

Ithaca frames its architecture explicitly as a collaborative, decision-support tool rather than an autonomous replacement for philologists (Assael et al. 2022, p. 280: "The architecture of Ithaca focuses on collaboration, decision support and interpretability"); Aeneas states this even more directly, emphasizing that AI should complement rather than substitute human expertise in the humanities (Assael et al. 2025, Ethics statement: "We also emphasize that AI should complement rather than substitute human expertise in the humanities"). Both systems report that access to model predictions measurably improves human performance: historians given Ithaca's top-20 restoration hypotheses improved their own restoration accuracy from 25% to 72% (Assael et al. 2022, p. 280: "the use of Ithaca by historians improved their accuracy from 25% to 72%"). We adopt the same framing — our goal is a tool that narrows the space of plausible readings and attributions for a human expert to evaluate.

## 2. Historical background

Enheduanna, high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur and daughter of Sargon of Akkad, founder of the Akkadian Empire, lived in the 23rd century BCE. For more than a millennium after the invention of writing (c. 3500 BCE), literature in Mesopotamia had been composed, copied, and circulated entirely anonymously; Enheduanna is the first individual in world history whose authorship of specific literary works is credited by name directly in the text (Helle 2019, p. 1: "Literature begins in complete anonymity. The oldest known literary texts are entirely anonymous. For more than a millennium after the invention of writing, c. 3500 BC, literature would be composed, copied, and circulated without authorial attributions... For the first time in world history, it became possible to link a work of poetry to a named individual, and authorship was born").

This claim carries an important caveat: the earliest surviving manuscript copies of her poems date mostly to the Old Babylonian period (19th–16th century BCE) — several centuries after her own lifetime — though two manuscripts of the Temple Hymns specifically are somewhat earlier, from the Third Dynasty of Ur and the early Isin period. Helle (2019) argues that her authorial prominence specifically crystallized in the city of Nippur after 1740 BCE, when Old Babylonian, Akkadian-speaking scribes — amid political crisis and social disruption — needed to assert privileged access to a prestigious but by then foreign Sumerian literary heritage (p. 2: "The condensation of Sumerian was particularly significant in the context where Enheduana's poems were most intensively studied: the city of Nippur in the decades after 1740 BC... in 1740 BC, Nippur was struck by political crises and social disruption. The city's scholars desperately needed to assert their importance, and so invoked the memory of a venerable Sumerian culture to which they held special access"). The figure of a named author served as a way to condense that heritage into a single, tangible, appropriable object (p. 8–9: "Enheduana came to serve as a body and a bridge for Sumerian identity").

Cuneiform itself was not devised for literature: for roughly its first half-millennium, writing in Mesopotamia was almost entirely restricted to administrative records, not a medium for religion, royal display, or storytelling, all of which existed without it (Cooper 2006, p. 83: "for the first half-millennium or so after its invention, writing was otherwise almost entirely restricted to administrative records... Writing in Babylonia emerged as an administrative technology: you can have religion and ritual without writing, you can have royal display without writing, but you cannot (easily) run a large-scale complex society without writing"). It was devised for Sumerian in southern Mesopotamia and later adopted by Akkadian speakers to the north, who took over the same script for a structurally very different language: Sumerian is a language isolate with no known relatives, while Akkadian belongs to the East Semitic branch (Woods 2006, p. 99: "the fact that Sumerian is an isolate... early Akkadian (which, along with Eblaite, is the only extant member of the eastern branch of the Semitic family)"). The most commonly cited view places Sumerian's effective death as a spoken vernacular by the Ur III period (c. 2100–2000 BCE) (Woods 2006, p. 92: "Both Cooper and Michalowski conclude... that Sumerian was essentially a dead or dying language by the Ur III period (ca. 2100–2000 B.C.)... That this is the prevailing view may be seen in the comments of Thomsen"), after which it continued to be used for roughly another two millennia purely as a written, scholarly, and liturgical language — a role Woods (2006) likens to Latin's afterlife in medieval Europe, though he is explicit the parallel is partial: Latin is the linguistic ancestor of the Romance vernaculars that eventually replaced it, whereas Sumerian and Akkadian are unrelated languages that had simply coexisted (p. 94–95: "written language and spoken language need not be identical; to find a well-known parallel, we need look no farther than the use of Latin in the middle ages and beyond... its relationship to the descendant Romance vernaculars is quite different from that which existed between Sumerian and Akkadian"). Exactly when Sumerian died as a spoken language is genuinely disputed among specialists — proposed dates range from the early third millennium to the middle of the second (Michalowski 2006, p. 160: "the death of the spoken vernacular language has been variously placed as early as the beginnings of the third millennium or as late as the middle of the second").

This history — not merely a labeling convenience — is why the corpus's own `language` head needs four categories rather than a simple binary: Akkadian and Sumerian as the two primary written languages spanning the corpus's full third-to-first-millennium timespan; Bilingual for texts that genuinely mix both; and Peripheral/Other for the languages of neighboring cultures that borrowed the same cuneiform script for their own tongues (Hittite, Elamite, Urartian, and others). Enheduanna's own case sits precisely at this fault line: composed in a Sumerian that would be a dead scholarly language by the time Old Babylonian, Akkadian-speaking scribes made her its symbolic author.

## 3. Related work

Machine learning applied to ancient-language tasks has grown rapidly in recent years, across scripts, tasks, and modalities (Sommerschield et al. 2023, p. 705: "As can be seen, the last 5 years have seen a substantial increase in the number of publications"); we organize the work most directly relevant to our own system below.

### 3.1. Fine-tuned BERT for Akkadian

Lazar et al. (2021) fine-tuned multilingual BERT (mBERT) on transliterated Akkadian text from ORACC, roughly 10,000 transliterated tablets totalling about 1 million words and 2.3 million signs (p. 4683: "Oracc consists of about 10K texts... containing 1M words and 2.3M signs"). Predicting masked signs, their fine-tuned model reached 89.5% top-5 accuracy (MRR 0.83, Hit@5 0.89 overall) (p. 4686, Table 2). Notably, mBERT's zero-shot performance (before any Akkadian finetuning at all) already outperformed a monolingual Akkadian BERT trained from scratch by about 10% (p. 4683: "the zero-shot performance of a multilingual language model surpasses that of a monolingual Akkadian model by about 10%"). To inject Akkadian-specific vocabulary without retraining mBERT's embedding table, they used 99 of mBERT's reserved but otherwise-unused vocabulary slots via the WordPiece algorithm (p. 4685: "To adapt M-BERT to Akkadian, we assign its 99 available free tokens, optimizing for maximum likelihood by the WordPiece tokenization algorithm"). We adopt the same mechanism for two damage-marker tokens (Section 5) and, going further, use the remaining free slots for 97 Akkadian-specific WordPiece tokens learned directly from our own corpus.

Fetaya et al. (2020) provide direct evidence, on Akkadian administrative and legal texts, that bidirectional context is substantially more informative than left-only context for this task: an LSTM given the full surrounding context reached MRR 0.89, against MRR 0.754 for the same architecture given only the context preceding a gap (Fetaya et al. 2020, p. 22746, Table 2). This is the strongest available justification, within the Akkadian-restoration literature itself, for a bidirectional encoder such as BERT over an autoregressive, left-to-right decoder.

The general strategy of domain-adapting a pretrained BERT encoder for text attribution by period and region is not unique to Akkadian or to epigraphy: Lendvai et al. (2023) apply the same fine-tuned-BERT-for-attribution approach to unrelated pre-modern Slavic manuscripts, attributing century and region from text alone (p. 15–16: "We demonstrate unsupervised domain adaptation and supervised finetuning of BERT on these low-resource, historical Slavic variants, for the purposes of provenance attribution in terms of three downstream tasks: manuscript, century and copying region classification") — direct precedent, outside the epigraphic literature, for the period and provenience classification heads we add in Section 5.

### 3.2. Pythia → Ithaca → Aeneas

Pythia, a sequence-to-sequence BiLSTM operating on character and word representations jointly, was the first deep-learning system applied to ancient-text restoration; on a held-out set of Greek inscriptions it reached a character error rate (CER) of 30.1%, against 57.3% for expert epigraphists working under the same conditions (Assael et al. 2019, p. 6368, 6371: "PYTHIA's predictions achieve a 30.1% character error rate, compared to the 57.3% of human epigraphists").

Ithaca restructured the task around one shared transformer torso feeding three separate task heads — restoration, geographic attribution, and chronological attribution — trained jointly on the Packard Humanities Institute's ancient Greek inscription corpus, 178,551 inscriptions (Assael et al. 2022, p. 281: "The final output of the torso is passed to three different task heads that handle restoration, geographical attribution and chronological attribution, respectively"; corpus: "the transcribed texts of 178,551 inscriptions"). Ithaca alone reaches 61.8% top-1 restoration accuracy (26.3% CER); for geographic attribution, among 84 candidate regions, 70.8% top-1 and 82.1% top-3 accuracy; for chronological attribution, its predictions fall a median of 3 years from the ground-truth date range (p. 282: "Ithaca consistently outperforms the competing methods, scoring a 26.3% CER and 61.8% top 1 accuracy... Regarding the attribution to regions, Ithaca has 70.8% top 1 and 82.1% top 3 predictive accuracy. Finally, for chronological attribution... a median distance of only 3 years"). This shared-torso, per-task-head design is the direct architectural precedent for the multi-task model described in Section 5.

Aeneas replaced Ithaca's torso with a T5-style decoder augmented with rotary positional embeddings (Assael et al. 2025, p. 142: "The torso is a deep narrow T5 transformer decoder that is augmented with relative positional rotary embeddings"), extended restoration to gaps of unknown length via a dedicated `#` symbol (p. 142: "two special characters are utilized—'-' indicates that the number of characters to restore is known, whereas '#' signals that it is unknown"), and added a vision branch: a small convolutional network (ResNet-8) processes a photograph of the inscription, and its output is concatenated with the text representation feeding the geographic-attribution head only (p. 142/150: "The visual inputs are processed using a ResNet-8 neural network. The resulting outputs are concatenated with the relevant textual embeddings and jointly processed by the geographical attribution head"). Aeneas's authors explicitly exclude the visual input from the restoration head, to avoid the model exploiting unmasked regions of the image to infer masked text, and from the dating head, having found no measurable benefit in ablation experiments (p. 142: "The visual input was excluded for the restoration task to prevent unintended information 'leakage'... the model would exploit visual cues to infer and restore the hidden characters. The visual modality was also omitted for the dating task because experiments showed no significant performance gains"). This scoping decision is the direct precedent for the vision-conditioned head described in Section 5; we did not simply adopt it, but independently re-derived the same scoping via our own controlled ablation (Section 6). Aeneas's full corpus (LED) totals 176,861 inscriptions, of which photographs were obtained for 5% — about 8,843 (p. 142: "The final LED comprises 176,861 inscriptions... we were able to obtain corresponding images of 5% of inscriptions").

### 3.3. Vision + cuneiform

Kapon et al. (2024) classify a cuneiform tablet's historical period from its photograph alone — no text — with a ResNet50 model trained on 94,936 CDLI tablet images, reaching 61% macro-F1 against an 8% baseline from a decision tree using only the tablet's height-to-width ratio ("Our classification approach begins with a Decision Tree using the height-to-width ratios of the tablets as the sole predictor which achieved 8% macro F1-score, and culminates with a ResNet50 model, which achieved a 61% macro-F1 score for tablet silhouettes"). This shows that a tablet's physical shape carries a real, learnable period signal through a purely visual channel. Our own results (Section 6) find no corresponding benefit from adding a visual input to a joint text+image model's period head; one plausible reconciliation is that text alone already captures most of the available period signal, leaving little for a fused image feature to add — unlike Kapon et al.'s vision-only setting, where the image is the model's only available signal.

Dencker et al. (2020) show that individual cuneiform signs can be localized within tablet photographs using only weak supervision — aligning existing transliterations to tablet images, rather than requiring manually annotated bounding boxes (p. 1: "we propose a weakly supervised approach: We align tablet images with their corresponding transliterations to localize the transliterated signs in the tablet image, before using these localized signs in place of annotations to re-train the sign detector").

Verwimp et al. (2025) train and evaluate a ResNet50 classifier for individual Old Babylonian cuneiform sign images, drawn from tablets across three Mesopotamian findspots (Nippur, Dūr-Abiešuḫ, Sippar), reaching 87.1% top-1 and 96.5% top-5 accuracy for signs with at least 20 training instances (p. 1). Their central finding, that classification performance degrades substantially when a model trained on one digitization source or scribal hand is applied to another (p. 1: "This variability makes it unlikely that an ML model trained on one dataset will perform successfully on another dataset"; illustrated concretely — sign SZU: 75.0% on Nippur material vs. 47.9%/56.7% on other findspots; sign UD: 92.5% vs. 22.2%/14.09%), is a direct caution for any cuneiform vision model — including ours — trained on a corpus assembled from multiple photographic sources.

Chen et al. (2024) train both text-based and image-based classifiers for cuneiform period dating on the same CuneiML corpus our own vision branch draws on, and identify a failure mode directly relevant to any model trained on tablets pooled across museum collections, including ours: label shift (different collections skew toward different time periods) compounded by covariate shift (different collections' own scanning methodologies leave identifiable artifacts), together driving a large accuracy gap between tablets from collections seen during training (up to 84.42%) and tablets from held-out collections (p. 30, 32: "On tablets from the same museum collections represented in the training set, we achieve accuracies as high as 84.42%. However, when test tablets are taken from held-out collections, models generalize more poorly... the scanning methodologies used by separate museums leave artifacts like different amounts of color saturation and blurring"), only partially closed by adversarial regularization and domain adaptation.

Williams et al. (2025) build DeepScribe, a two-stage pipeline (RetinaNet localization, ResNet classification) for locating and identifying individual Elamite cuneiform signs in photographs of the Persepolis Fortification Archive, reaching 0.78 localization mAP and 0.80 end-to-end top-5 sign classification accuracy (p. 1: "a RetinaNet object detector achieves a localization mAP of 0.78 and a ResNet classifier achieves a top-5 sign classification accuracy of 0.89. The end-to-end pipeline achieves a top-5 classification accuracy of 0.80") — a sign-level complement to Dencker's and Verwimp's own work, applied to a different cuneiform-writing language and period.

Hagelskjær (2026) approaches cuneiform tablet metadata classification from a different visual modality entirely — a 3D point cloud sampled from a CAD model rather than a 2D photograph (p. 1: "a point cloud is sampled from the CAD model") — outperforming a Point-BERT baseline on several classification tasks. 3D scanning captures information a flat photograph cannot (text wrapping around a tablet's edges), but remains far less scalable across the hundreds of thousands of excavated tablets than 2D photography, which is why we, like the large majority of work cited here, work from 2D photographs.

Mahmood and Panok (2025) apply neural machine translation to the reconstruction and translation of damaged Akkadian passages from the Epic of Gilgamesh, reporting that their system performs well on formulaic lines and passages with close parallels elsewhere in the epic, but struggles with rare vocabulary, damaged proper names, and syntactically uncertain lines (p. 99: "The AI system performs strongly on relatively formulaic lines and on passages with close parallels elsewhere in the epic... It struggles, however, with rare lexical items, damaged proper names, and lines whose syntactic structure is uncertain"). On our own curated literary examples (Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, the Laws of Hammurabi), top-1 restoration accuracy no longer shows a clear gap against a random sample of the (predominantly administrative) test set after this project's corpus rebuild and retrain — 66%/63% (text-only/vision) on 8 curated examples against 66%/67% on a 20-example random sample; with only 8 curated examples the comparison is noisy in either direction, so we report the current numbers as an open observation rather than a confirmed replication.

### 3.4. Restoration for other ancient languages

The transformer-based restoration approach we apply to Akkadian has close analogues for other under-resourced ancient languages and scripts, beyond the Greek/Latin lineage described above. Fono et al. (2024) are, to their own account, the first to apply a masked-language-modeling framing to damaged ancient Hebrew and Aramaic inscriptions, evaluating transformer models fine-tuned on Biblical text at several masking percentages and finding that an ensemble of word-level and character-level prediction heads outperforms either alone (p. 846: "This study is the first attempt to apply the masked language modeling approach to corrupted inscriptions in Hebrew and Aramaic languages... For any masking percentage, the highest text completion accuracy is obtained with a novel ensemble of word and character prediction models") — a multi-granularity combination our own single-token-level restoration does not attempt.

Gu et al. (2024) fine-tune RoBERTa for restoration of ancient Chinese texts, adding phrase-level semantic features and a KAN prediction layer on top of the base model (p. 96: "We propose an Ancient Chinese Text Restoration Model (ACTRM) that leverages the pre-trained RoBERTa model... The model's predictive capabilities are notably enhanced through the incorporation of phrase-level semantics within the input embedding layer and the application of a KAN network in the prediction layer") — an architecture choice directly parallel to our own use of a fine-tuned masked-language-model encoder rather than a from-scratch or autoregressive alternative.

Papavassileiou et al. (2023) apply a bidirectional recurrent generative model, benchmarked against n-gram baselines, to infill damaged and incomplete words in Mycenaean Greek texts written in the Linear B script — an Aegean writing system unrelated to cuneiform — verifying their method against ground truth before applying it to genuinely damaged tablets and comparing its output with expert philologists' own judgments (p. 52:1: "We verify our method experimentally using ground-truth, then we demonstrate our results on real cases and compare with experts' opinions") — the same historian-comparison framing Ithaca and Aeneas later adopt at much larger scale.

Levine et al. (2024) train a bidirectional RNN for character-level restoration of Coptic manuscripts, finding 72% single-character accuracy that falls to 37% for lacunae of varying length, and — rather than treating the model as a stand-alone restoration tool — use it to rank candidate reconstructions already under scholarly consideration ("Our best model performs with 72% accuracy on single character reconstruction, but falls to 37% when reconstructing lacunae of various lengths. While not suitable for definitive manuscript reconstruction, we argue that our RNN model can help scholars rank the likelihood of textual reconstructions") — an assistive framing close to our own decision-support goal (Section 1).

Hua et al. (2025) combine restoration and chronological attribution in one masked-language-modeling framework for early Chinese bronze inscriptions, adding an allograph-aware component that links distinct glyph forms of the same underlying grapheme ("We introduce BIRD... We further propose an allograph-aware masked language modeling framework that integrates domain- and task-adaptive pretraining with a Glyph Net (GN), which links graphemes and allographs") — a domain-specific extension with no direct analogue in our own WordPiece vocabulary, but a reminder that vocabulary and tokenization choices matter as much as architecture for restoration quality in these scripts.

### 3.5. Other Akkadian NLP

Several other recent projects apply deep learning to different Akkadian-language tasks: automatic transliteration of Unicode cuneiform glyphs into words, at up to 97% accuracy (Gordin et al. 2020, p. 1: "with an accuracy reaching up to 97% for automatically transliterating and segmenting standard Unicode cuneiform glyphs into words"); neural machine translation from Akkadian to English, from both cuneiform glyphs and transliteration directly (Gutherz et al. 2023, p. 1–2: "to automatically translate Akkadian from cuneiform Unicode glyphs directly to English (C2E) and from transliteration to English (T2E)"); and, most recently, bidirectional Akkadian-English translation paired with a tool for rendering composed text as cuneiform (Wang 2026, p. 132: "The system integrates a ByT5-based translation model trained on 116K bidirectional samples, a cuneiform sign converter with 14,240 mappings (95.3% coverage), and a visual tablet renderer"). None of these address restoration or attribution. A 2025 shared task on lemmatization and token prediction for Akkadian and Sumerian using LLMs (Gordin, Sahala, Spencer & Klein 2025, p. 164: "explores how Large Language Models (LLMs) and transformer-based models can be used to improve lemmatization and token prediction tasks for low-resource ancient cuneiform texts") further confirms that restoration-style prediction is an active benchmarking target for the field.

### 3.6. Data sources

Our corpus draws on two external sources beyond ORACC's own catalogue metadata. CuneiML supplies whole-tablet Unicode transliterations paired with CDLI photographs, using bounding-box face detection to crop the tablet's photographed face for the image input (Chen et al. 2023, p. 1: "tokenized and converted the Latin transliterations of Sumerian and Akkadian into cuneiform Unicode"; p. 5, §3.3: "We build and test three different computer vision methods to segment and obtain individual faces of each tablet") — this same face-cropping mechanism is the basis of the visual input used in Section 5. The electronic Babylonian Library (eBL) publishes a growing, openly licensed (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) transliteration corpus of roughly 25,000 tablets and 350,000 lines (Cobanoglu et al. 2024, p. 1, 5: "the constantly growing dataset contains around 25,000 tablets with over 350,000 lines of transliterated text"; "Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)"); we draw on the project's Zenodo snapshot both to recover transliterations for tablets missing usable text elsewhere and for several of our curated literary examples.

## 4. Data

**Evaluation regime.** We evaluate restoration under one regime: 15% of tokens masked at random following BERT's own masking recipe, evaluated on a held-out test split never used during training or checkpoint selection. Like Aeneas, we do not strip editorial conjectures — signs an ORACC editor restored inside square brackets — from the training text: the primary motivation for keeping them is the limited availability of data (Assael et al. 2025, p. 149: "The primary motivation for inclusion of this material was the limited availability of data").

**Corpus composition.** Our corpus totals 150,808 documents (train 135,626 / validation 7,664 / test 7,518). The majority, 144,754, come from merging and deduplicating ORACC's own editions with CuneiML's; a further 6,054 are drawn directly from CDLI's bulk ATF export and from eBL — recovering tablets whose text existed nowhere else in our sources, balancing underrepresented period/genre/language/provenience classes, and adding a curated set of well-known literary texts (the Epic of Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, the Laws of Hammurabi, and — fittingly, given this system's own name — Enheduanna's Exaltation of Inanna, Temple Hymns, and dedicatory disc) held out into the test split. 12,626 documents (8.4%) carry a real tablet photograph, reviewed by hand for a correct bounding box; the remainder receive an all-zero placeholder image at training time. Every photographed document is presented to the model, but — as detailed in Section 5 — it is used by only one of the four classification heads, provenience.

**Metadata categories.** We map the free-text period, genre, provenience, and language fields recorded by CDLI and ORACC onto four small, fixed category sets, coarsening CDLI's own finer distinctions to keep each head's class count tractable at our corpus size — our genre category "Literary & Scholarly", for instance, merges CDLI's astrological, omen, ritual, mathematical, and prayer genres. The final sets: nine periods (Neo-Assyrian, Ur III, Old Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian, Middle Babylonian, Neo-Babylonian, Third Millennium, Late Antiquity); six genres (Administrative, Lexical, Royal Inscriptions, Literary & Scholarly, Legal, Letters); four languages (Akkadian, Sumerian, Bilingual, Peripheral/Other); thirty-six provenience classes, from large well-known sites (Nineveh, Babylon, Ḫattusa, Mari, Ebla, Susa, ...) to smaller but well-attested findspots — a label space comparable in scale to Ithaca's and Aeneas's own geographic-attribution classes (84 and 62 regions respectively). A handful of period and provenience candidate classes considered during development (Middle Hittite, Proto-/Neo-/Middle Elamite for period; nine additional findspots for provenience) were dropped after finding fewer than 50 actual recoverable documents behind their catalogue-scale counts.

**Missing labels.** A record whose raw value matches none of these categories, or is simply absent, is treated as missing: it is assigned label -100 and excluded from both the loss and the accuracy/F1 computation for that head, rather than defaulted to an arbitrary class. Missing-label rates: 14.8% for period, 19.5% for genre, 22.4% for language, 21.9% for provenience — reflecting how consistently each field is actually populated in the underlying catalogues; language, the least completely populated of the four, is the most affected.

**Text-level damage markers.** Separately from missing category labels, the transliterated text itself carries two distinct damage signals, which we map to two of mBERT's reserved token slots and exclude from both masking targets and the model's own candidate outputs, since a position marking genuinely unrecoverable content has no ground truth to train against: a single sign of uncertain reading (`x` in the source transliteration, present in 29.6% of documents and 5.94% of word-tokens), and a gap of unknown length (an ellipsis in the source transliteration, present in 28.8% of documents and 1.42% of characters).

## 5. Methods

Our encoder is `bert-base-multilingual-cased`, fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch: the available Akkadian corpus is orders of magnitude smaller than what a competitive from-scratch transformer requires, and Aeneas's own corpus, substantially larger than ours, is itself described by its authors as "multiple orders of magnitude smaller than recent NLP datasets" (Assael et al. 2025, p. 149) — the same scale argument applies to us more strongly still. We confirm this empirically for our own corpus (Section 7): masked-token-prediction mean reciprocal rank rises from 0.481, for an otherwise identical but un-finetuned model, to 0.865 after finetuning.

Four classification heads — period, genre, language, provenience — are trained jointly with the masked-language-modeling objective on one shared encoder, following the same shared-torso, per-task-head design as Ithaca and Aeneas (Section 3.2), extended from their two attribution tasks (region, date) to four; genre and language have no direct equivalent in Ithaca/Aeneas's Greek/Latin setting. Task losses are weighted 3.0 for the masked-language-modeling objective and 1.0 for each metadata head — close to, though not copied from, Aeneas's own fixed weighting of 3 for restoration, 2 for region, and 1.25 for date (Assael et al. 2025, p. 149: "L = 3·L_restoration + L_unknown + 2·L_region + 1.25·L_date").

For tablets with a photograph, we add a vision branch: a ResNet18 backbone, initialized from ImageNet and fine-tuned jointly with the rest of the model, whose output is normalized and concatenated with the encoder's `[CLS]` representation feeding the provenience head only. This mirrors Aeneas's design of concatenating a small convolutional network's output into the geographic-attribution head alone (Section 3.2), with two differences. First, we use a larger, ImageNet-pretrained backbone rather than a from-scratch ResNet-8: a from-scratch convolutional network overfit in preliminary experiments given our own photographed-document count (12,626 — now exceeding Aeneas's own roughly 8,843, their reported 5% of 176,861 inscriptions — even though our overall document corpus, 150,808, is still somewhat smaller than theirs). Second, we use lighter geometric image augmentation than Aeneas's own zooming/rotation/brightness-contrast augmentation (p. 149), after finding a wider range clipped genuine tablet content on our tightly, manually reviewed image crops; Aeneas does not publish specific angle values for its own augmentation, so we do not offer a direct numeric comparison here, only a qualitative one.

**Similar-document retrieval.** Following Aeneas's own contextualization mechanism, we build a single embedding per document from the trained encoder's own hidden states, with no separate retrieval model. Aeneas defines its embedding as

`emb_context = ( emb_torso[t=1] + (1/N) · Σ_{t=2..N} emb_torso[t] ) / 2`

where `emb_torso[t=1]`, the torso's first output, "aligns with the sentence prefix" and the remaining `t=2..N` outputs align with the textual input (Assael et al. 2025, p. 150). Our encoder has no such prefix token to repurpose, but `[CLS]` plays exactly the same summary role by construction, so we substitute it directly:

`emb_doc = ( h_[CLS] + (1/(L-1)) · Σ_{i=2..L} h_i ) / 2`

for a sequence of `L` tokens with encoder outputs `h_1 = h_[CLS], h_2, ..., h_L`. Aeneas's authors report that this simple, un-trained embedding outperformed several trained retrieval alternatives (embedding texts and metadata jointly, or as raw inputs) in their own preliminary evaluation, attributing this to their corpus's limited size (Assael et al. 2025, p. 150: "owing to the limited dataset size, our preliminary evaluation revealed that similarity scoring with Aeneas' embeddings yielded the most relevant inscriptions"); our corpus is smaller still, so we adopt the same design rather than building a dedicated retrieval model. Retrieval itself uses cosine similarity between a query document's embedding and every other document's, over the full corpus (train, validation, and test alike).

**Saliency maps.** For explainability, we adopt Aeneas's own choice of gradient-based saliency (the "vanilla gradient" method of Simonyan et al. 2014, p. 4: "the saliency map is obtained by rearranging the elements of the vector w... the map can be computed as Mij = |wh(i,j)|"). The reliability of saliency maps as an explanation method remains generally disputed (Assael et al. 2025, p. 150: "the effectiveness of saliency maps remains an active topic of discussion"), though Aeneas's own team still found them a valuable tool, particularly for textual inputs. For a given prediction — a restored token at a masked position, or a classification head's predicted class — we backpropagate from that single output logit to the token input embeddings and take the L2 norm of the gradient at each position, giving a per-token saliency score with no retraining required. Unlike Aeneas, which reports one saliency map per task, we produce one per head, plus one per restored position, so a reader can inspect exactly which context tokens drove any single prediction. For the vision-conditioned provenience head specifically, we additionally compute a Grad-CAM map — a gradient-based class-activation localization over a CNN's last convolutional block (Selvaraju et al. 2017, p. 1: "uses the gradients of any target concept... flowing into the final convolutional layer to produce a coarse localization map highlighting the important regions in the image") — over the ResNet18 backbone's last convolutional block, highlighting which region of the photograph most influenced the predicted provenience.

**Restoration error metric (CER).** Alongside the token-level metrics above, we report a pooled character-level error rate over every masked position in the evaluation split:

`CER = ( Σ_i edit_distance(pred_i, target_i) ) / ( Σ_i len(target_i) )`

where `pred_i`/`target_i` are the top-1 predicted and true WordPiece token's surface string at masked position `i`. Our masking is standard BERT-style random-15%-of-tokens MLM, with no designed span-length control, so we report one pooled ratio rather than stratifying by masked-span length the way Ithaca and Aeneas stratify their own CER — their masking has a controlled span length (1–20 characters), ours does not, so a direct stratification does not apply.

## 6. Results

**Metrics.** We report mean reciprocal rank, top-1/top-3/top-5 accuracy, and a pooled character error rate (CER, defined in Section 5) for masked-token restoration — the first three matching Lazar et al. (2021)'s own metric choice (p. 4686) for direct comparability — and accuracy alongside macro-averaged F1 for each of the four classification heads.

**Headline results.** On the held-out test split, the vision-conditioned checkpoint — the model we report as this project's deliverable — reaches a masked-token restoration MRR of 0.865 (top-1/top-3/top-5 accuracy 0.820/0.895/0.919, CER 19.6%), and macro-F1 of 0.895 (period), 0.908 (genre), 0.839 (language), and 0.685 (provenience). The text-only comparison isolates the image's own contribution: provenience macro-F1 rises from 0.674 to 0.685 (+0.011) with vision, while the other three heads move by comparable or smaller amounts in either direction (period 0.880→0.895, genre 0.904→0.908, language 0.843→0.839) — restoration itself is essentially unaffected (CER 19.5% vs 19.6%), as expected, since the image only ever reaches the provenience head.

**Why only provenience benefits from vision.** A controlled ablation — six checkpoints, each conditioning a different subset of heads on the image (provenience alone, in two independent reruns; period alone; genre alone; period+genre; and the text-only control) — confirms this is not incidental: only the provenience head's image-conditioned score clears the range it shows across every run where it is not image-conditioned, while period's and genre's own apparent movements stay inside their own unconditioned range — indistinguishable from ordinary run-to-run noise. A plausible reason is that a tablet's photograph carries information tied specifically to where and how it was found and later photographed — clay color and condition, damage pattern, a given collection's own photography setup — a signal tied to provenience specifically, whereas period, genre, and language are already largely recoverable from the text itself, leaving the image little to add for those three heads.

**Per-class breakdown.** Movement at the individual provenience-class level is noisy in both directions, not concentrated only in the smallest classes: Tuttul gains +0.292 (17 test examples) and Hattusa +0.222 (4), while Nimrud loses −0.077 on a much larger 77-example support, and Pī-Kasî (−0.222, 6) and Susa (−0.214, 10) lose on small ones — consistent with per-class sample size, not a uniform effect, dominating the class-level picture at this scale.

**Qualitative examples.** A worked example — tablet P387407, with its photograph, cuneiform, and transliteration — and a restoration example for the Kukkulanum and Lamassī tablets (test split; the full, unedited model output for all seven examples is kept in a separate results file for this project).

## 7. Conclusion

We present Enheduanna, to our knowledge the first system to combine Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution with masked-token restoration and a vision-conditioned head for Akkadian. Where Lazar et al. (2021) established restoration alone for this language, and Aeneas established vision-conditioned attribution for Latin at a substantially larger data scale, we combine both directions and add two attribution tasks — genre and language — with no direct analogue in prior epigraphic systems. Our central empirical finding — that a tablet's photograph offers the vision-conditioned model a consistent, if modest, edge over the text-only one on provenience attribution — is broadly consistent with Aeneas's own finding that vision conditioning benefits geographic attribution specifically, for a structurally different language, script, and corpus scale; our own margin is narrower, and noisier at the individual-class level, than Aeneas's reported results, which we read as a genuine property of the underlying data and evaluation scale rather than a claim we can isolate as cleanly as Aeneas does. We also report, without omission, that an alternative from-scratch character-level architecture was built and evaluated for this project and found unproductive relative to fine-tuned mBERT at comparable training cost.
